EXPANDED Official U.S. Oct 26, 2026

Treasury proposes rules for the outbound investment law (COINS Act): a wider list of countries and technologies, with some deals banned and others requiring notice

U.S. Treasury, Office of Investment Security

What changed: Treasury published a proposed rule (91 FR 67396; RIN 1505-AC94; docket TREAS-DO-2026-0595) to implement the COINS Act, which Congress passed in the FY2026 NDAA on Dec 18, 2025. It would create a new 31 CFR Part 851 to replace the Part 850 program that has applied since Jan 2, 2025.

U.S. persons (citizens, green-card holders, U.S. entities and their foreign branches) would have to notify Treasury of some investments, and would be barred from others, in 'covered foreign persons' tied to countries of concern that work in listed technologies. Covered deals include buying equity or contingent equity, certain convertible or equity-like loans, joint ventures, greenfield projects, directing a foreign person's deal, and some limited-partner stakes in non-U.S. funds. U.S. parents would also answer for deals made by foreign subsidiaries they control. The countries of concern grow from China (including Hong Kong and Macau) to also cover Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia and Venezuela 'under the regime of Nicolas Maduro', which Treasury says no longer exists. The technologies grow from semiconductors, quantum and AI (renamed 'super intelligence systems') to add high-performance computing/supercomputing and hypersonics; Treasury's press release also lists biotechnology. The statute exempts bank lending, payment processing and clearing, underwriting, prime brokerage and global custody, plus de minimis deals at amounts Treasury has not yet set. National-interest exemptions are available.

“prohibit United States persons from engaging in certain other transactions”
  • Control Prohibition
  • Affects Sector or group
  • Stage Proposal
  • Tier 1 (#3)
  • Direction Toward controls
  • Evidence Official document

Why it matters: This is the only live federal framework that requires advance notice for, or outright bans, Americans' investments abroad. It now rests on a statute instead of an executive order, so it is more durable. The proposal widens the countries and sectors covered and lets Treasury add more technology categories through notice and comment. It also reaches indirect routes (fund-of-funds and LP stakes, structures that hide a U.S. person's role) and makes 'reasonable and diligent inquiry' a compliance duty.

Why it might not: It is targeted at national security. It covers only specified technology activities by entities linked to six named countries, and it does not limit ordinary payments, bank lending, custody or deposits. Treasury reports only about 100 notifications in roughly 21 months of the existing program. For most Americans it limits nothing about moving or holding money.

NEW Official U.S. Nov 4, 2026

FDIC proposal would move the largest banks under the 2008 'provisional hold' rule, for use if they fail

FDIC

What changed: The FDIC Board approved the proposal Oct 30; it was published Nov 4, with comments due Jan 4, 2027. It would rescind 12 CFR Part 370 and move banks with at least $2 billion in deposits and 2 million or more accounts under an amended 12 CFR 360.9.

This is mainly a deregulatory proposal: the largest banks would no longer have to calculate deposit insurance in-house. Instead they would supply standardized depositor data and, within 18 months of the rule taking effect, be able to place and remove automated provisional holds on accounts if they fail, which other large banks have had to support since 2008. Holds apply only after a failure, while the FDIC separates insured from uninsured money. Some pass-through account holders, such as deposit brokers and agents, would have to be told they might not reach those deposits after a failure until they give the FDIC the required data.

“might not have access to deposits before the account holder delivers information”
  • Control Friction
  • Affects Sector or group
  • Stage Proposal
  • Tier 2 (#10)
  • Direction Neutral
  • Evidence Official document

Why it matters: It makes the plumbing for temporarily restricting depositor access standard at the very largest banks, so it's worth tracking even though it's tied to bank failures.

Why it might not: Holds are used only after a bank fails and is in FDIC receivership, to sort insured from uninsured money while paying depositors quickly. The mechanism isn't new, the proposal mainly reduces burden, and it isn't linked to any crisis policy.

ADVANCED Official U.S. Oct 21, 2026

GENIUS Act rules move forward: OCC final rule adopted, Fed proposals open for comment until Nov 30, Treasury proposal on U.S. issuance and sales

OCC; Federal Reserve Board; U.S. Treasury

What changed: The OCC adopted its final GENIUS rule (new 12 CFR Part 15) on Oct 21, 2026, effective Jan 18, 2027. The Fed proposed reserve, capital and application rules on Sept 24, 2026, with comments due Nov 30. Treasury's section 3 proposal (2026-16796) closed for comment on Oct 19, 2026.

From Jan 18, 2027, only licensed issuers may issue payment stablecoins in the U.S. Issuers must back coins fully with reserves such as short-term Treasury bills and meet redemption, capital and risk standards. Starting July 18, 2028, digital-asset service providers may not offer U.S. persons stablecoins from issuers that are not permitted. The OCC set BSA, AML and sanctions requirements aside for a separate rulemaking with Treasury. Treasury's proposal defines 'issue' to exclude transfers 'required by a lawful order'.

“fully back their stablecoins with certain permissible reserve assets”
  • Control Permission
  • Affects Sector or group
  • Stage Adopted
  • Tier 1 (#6)
  • Direction Neutral
  • Evidence Official document

Why it matters: This is building a regulated private digital-dollar system in which every compliant token carries freeze capability. It requires reserves in short-term Treasuries, which creates captive demand for government debt but only within the issuer sector.

Why it might not: It is licensing and prudential regulation of issuers. Holders face no new limits on withdrawing, redeeming or moving funds, and the rules include redemption standards.

NEW Official U.S. Oct 30, 2026

FinCEN alert asks banks to watch cross-border payments sent through U.S. money transmitters at a 'foreign agent's' direction

FinCEN / Treasury (White House Task Force to Eliminate Fraud)

What changed: On Oct 30, 2026 in Minnesota, FinCEN issued an alert on 'foreign-agent-directed transactions' through U.S. money services businesses and a notice on abuse of charities and nonprofits. Treasury also announced a fraud whistleblower reward program.

The alert asks financial institutions to spot and report, through suspicious activity reports, cross-border payments in which U.S. MSBs are used to hide who ultimately receives the money and why. Secretary Bessent said FinCEN found more than $8 billion in potentially suspicious foreign agent-directed payments from 2021 to 2025. The companion notice describes sham-charity, fraud and embezzlement schemes.

EASED Counter-signal Oct 30, 2026

Bank regulators bar 'reputation risk' debanking; the OCC's final debanking report finds subjective account-access policies at the largest banks

OCC; FDIC; CFPB (follow-up)

What changed: The OCC/FDIC final rule (2026-06947, published Apr 10, 2026) carries out EO 14331, 'Guaranteeing Fair Banking for All Americans'. Around late October 2026 the OCC issued its final debanking report on the nine largest national banks; the exact release date is unconfirmed. The CFPB says it will investigate the banks named.

The rule stops the OCC and FDIC from criticizing or acting against banks over reputation risk. It also stops them from pushing banks to close or deny accounts because of a customer's political, religious or other views, or because the customer runs a lawful but disfavored business. Sanctions-related rejections are still required. The OCC report found that from 2020 to September 2025 large banks used reputation-risk and industry policies that lacked objective criteria. Industries affected included firearms, coal, oil and gas, detention facilities, tobacco and digital assets.

“codify the elimination of reputation risk from their supervisory programs”
  • Affects All Americans
  • Stage In effect
  • Direction Away from controls
  • Evidence Official document

Why it matters: It strengthens Americans' ability to keep and open bank accounts regardless of viewpoint or lawful industry.

Why it might not: It binds only the agencies, not the banks directly; the Fed's parallel rule was proposed Feb 23, 2026 and its final status is unconfirmed. BSA and sanctions-based account closures are not affected.

Next: Fed final reputation-risk rule; CFPB investigations (dates unconfirmed)

NEW International Oct 24, 2026

Canada publishes draft Stablecoin Regulations; consultation open to Dec 23

Department of Finance Canada / Bank of Canada

What changed: Draft regulations under the Stablecoin Act (Royal Assent Mar 26, 2026) were published for a 60-day consultation; the Bank of Canada also opened consultation on draft supervisory guidelines on Nov 2.

Canada is building a licensing regime for fiat-backed stablecoins similar in spirit to the U.S. GENIUS Act: registration, full reserves, redemption at par, no interest. Most of the Act is expected in force in 2027. The framework covers foreign-currency (including U.S. dollar) stablecoins offered to Canadians. It is a supervision regime, not a restriction on individuals' holdings.

“60-day consultation (until December 23, 2026”

Money market fund rules today: redemption gates are gone, but some funds must charge liquidity fees under heavy outflows
Details

The 2023 amendments removed fund boards' power to temporarily suspend redemptions (gates) and raised minimum daily and weekly liquid assets to 25% and 50%. Institutional prime and institutional tax-exempt funds must charge a liquidity fee when daily net redemptions exceed 5% of net assets, unless the cost is de minimis. Other non-government funds may charge discretionary fees. Government and retail funds can use share cancellation in a negative-rate environment.

Why it matters: A mandatory exit fee triggered by stress is a standing friction on redemptions. Any proposal to bring back gates or extend fees to government or retail funds would be a meaningful escalation.

Why it might not: Gates were abolished, which is a move away from controls. Fees apply only to institutional prime and tax-exempt funds, and most retail sweep and government funds are not subject to them. Nothing changed in the past 90 days.

Details

What would raise the level

Specific next steps to watch for.

  • A cap on, or a government approval requirement for, individual transfers abroad, beyond sanctions and national-security investment screening.
  • H.R. 8995, or any similar bill raising the remittance tax or extending it to bank- and card-funded transfers, getting a hearing, a markup or a ride on a must-pass bill.
  • FinCEN extending cross-border reporting orders to more regions, lowering thresholds, renewing them repeatedly or making them permanent, or a rule implementing FATF's payment-transparency standard that adds source-of-funds or approval steps for ordinary transfers abroad.
  • Any regulator discussing temporary limits on deposit withdrawals or bank holidays, bringing back money-fund redemption gates, or extending liquidity fees to government or retail funds, or officials applying terms like 'temporary liquidity restrictions' or 'capital flow management' to U.S. deposits, stablecoins or money funds.
  • FDIC provisional holds being used at a large bank failure, a cluster of failures in a short period, or several large banks adding unexplained withdrawal or wire limits (confirmed by banks or regulators, not social media).
  • Final stablecoin rules allowing freezes without a specific lawful order, extending freeze duties to all secondary-market holders, restricting self-hosted wallets, or a mandatory (not voluntary) hold regime for crypto withdrawals.

  • Requirements for retirement accounts, money funds or banks to hold set shares of Treasury debt, or new limits on retirement withdrawals, rollovers, or foreign accounts and assets.
  • Repeal or narrowing of the statutory CBDC bar, a retail CBDC pilot, or a digital euro holding cap cited by U.S. officials as a model.
  • The final outbound-investment rule widening to publicly traded securities, index funds, or more countries or technologies.

About this report

What counts: limits on Americans' ability to withdraw, move, convert, invest or hold money. A routine anti-fraud, sanctions, anti-money-laundering or tax-reporting rule isn't a capital control by itself, so each item says what the measure actually lets the government or a bank do.

Control ladder: Visibility (reporting) → Friction (delays, extra checks, fees, taxes) → Permission (government approval needed) → Prohibition (ban, limit, freeze, forced conversion).

Signal tiers: Tier 1 is subtle (new reporting, cash-reporting changes, outbound-investment limits, new official vocabulary, proposed emergency powers, payment systems that enable monitoring or conditions). Tier 2 is concerning (limits on transfers abroad or currency conversion, unexplained withdrawal limits, regulators discussing deposit limits, holding periods or redemption gates, limits on foreign assets, retirement-account limits). Tier 3 is serious (government withdrawal limits, broad bans on moving money overseas, forced conversion, bank holidays, emergency orders).

Alert levels: 🟢 GREEN means quiet. 🟡 YELLOW means subtle movement. 🟠 ORANGE means an official proposal for a Tier 2 measure, or one adopted for a defined group. 🔴 RED means a Tier 3 measure is adopted, imminent or in effect for ordinary Americans. Measures limited to failed banks, sanctioned parties or named countries count toward YELLOW, not ORANGE. Commentary and unverified claims can't raise the level.

How it's made: researched and compiled each morning with an AI assistant from public sources. It can contain errors, so check the linked sources before relying on anything.
