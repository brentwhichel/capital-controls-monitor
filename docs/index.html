The short version

What are capital controls?

Capital controls are rules that limit what people can do with their money. For example, a limit on how much cash you can take out of the bank, or a ban on sending money to another country. Some countries use them during money emergencies. This page watches for any signs of them in the United States.

Today's level: 🟡 Yellow (small changes, keep an eye out)

Nothing stops regular people in the U.S. from taking out, moving or saving their money. A bank failed in Paraguay, but that doesn't limit Americans' money.

Should I worry?

Not right now. A plan to track smaller money transfers to other countries is now officially dropped. A big bank failed in Paraguay, a country in South America, and its savers are being paid back in steps. We found no limits on taking out or moving money in the U.S.

What would make the level go up?

  • A cap on how much money people can send to other countries.
  • Limits on how much cash people can take out of the bank.
  • An order that freezes or blocks money for most people, not just a few named companies.

Paraguay's central bank takes over failed digital lender Ueno Bank; accounts are frozen while a state fund repays savers in stages

Banco Central del Paraguay (Paraguay's central bank) and its banking and securities supervisors

What changed: On Nov 2, 2026, Paraguay's central bank voted unanimously to put Ueno Bank into resolution (a state-run wind-down) after the bank's solvency ratio fell below half the legal minimum. Ueno's services stopped early on Nov 3, locking customers out of their accounts except for payouts from the state Deposit Guarantee Fund, which began the night of Nov 5. By Nov 8, 64% of Ueno's 1,867,531 depositor accounts, almost 1.2 million, had been paid a total of Gs 994.6 billion (about $158 million), and the central bank said it would start paying depositors who also owe Ueno money on Nov 9, after subtracting their overdue debts. On Nov 6, Paraguay's Senate removed President Santiago Peña from office over his links to the bank.

Ueno launched as a fintech in 2021 and began operating as a bank in November 2023; it says it had 2.9 million customers, around 60% of Paraguay's adults. The guarantee fund covers up to 75 monthly minimum wages per depositor (Gs 228.3 million, about $38,000). The first payments, an advance of up to 40 minimum wages (Gs 121.76 million), went to depositors with no debts at Ueno and an account at another bank; savings certificates and larger balances have no payment date yet. Paraguay's securities regulator, part of the central bank, briefly suspended withdrawals from some mutual funds that held Ueno investments while their value was checked, and began lifting those pauses on Nov 4 and 5. The central bank says the problem is particular to Ueno: other banks' ATMs, branches and online banking kept working, though some banking apps slowed as people rushed to open new accounts, and it called social-media rumors of takeovers at other institutions false. Prosecutors are investigating the collapse, and Paraguay's state pension fund had about $355 million at Ueno.

Why it matters: It shows in real time what a large bank failure means for savers: accounts frozen, insured money returned in stages over several days, and a regulator briefly pausing withdrawals from investment funds tied to the bank. That is the kind of situation U.S. failed-bank rules, such as the FDIC's pending 'provisional hold' proposal for the largest banks, are designed to handle.

Why it might not: It is one failed bank in another country, handled under Paraguay's own deposit-guarantee law, and the fund pauses lasted only days. In the U.S., deposits are insured to at least $250,000 at each FDIC-insured bank, and the FDIC's list shows no U.S. bank failures since Sept 25.

FinCEN and the Fed drop a 2020 plan to cut the record-keeping threshold for transfers abroad from $3,000 to $250

FinCEN (Treasury) and the Federal Reserve Board

What changed: FinCEN and the Fed announced on Nov 6, 2026, that they were withdrawing their October 2020 joint proposal (85 FR 68005; RIN 1506-AB41). The withdrawal notice (FR Doc 2026-22809) was published in the Federal Register on Nov 9, making the withdrawal official.

The 2020 proposal would have lowered, from $3,000 to $250, the amount at which banks and money transmitters must collect and keep sender and recipient details on funds transfers that begin or end outside the U.S., and the amount at which FinCEN's travel rule requires passing those details along the payment chain. It also would have stated that these rules cover crypto and digital assets with legal-tender status. The agencies said they received nearly 3,000 comments, mostly opposed; that more than five years had passed; and that crypto markets and laws, including the GENIUS Act, have changed. They said no similar final rule will be issued without a new chance for public comment. The $3,000 threshold stays in place.

Why it matters: It retires a long-pending plan that would have brought far more everyday transfers abroad, such as family remittances, under record-keeping and pass-along requirements, and the agencies committed to new public comment before trying anything similar.

Why it might not: The existing $3,000 rules stay in place, and the agencies left room for future changes aimed at crypto. Tighter reporting can still come through other tools, such as geographic targeting orders like the Minnesota and Southwest border orders, or rules to carry out FATF's new payment-transparency standard.

Next: None scheduled; the agencies said any similar final rule would first get a new chance for public comment

Taiwan's short-selling limits and South Korea's margin-loan relief take effect after Asian stock markets fell up to 6% following the U.S. drop

Taiwan's Financial Supervisory Commission and National Financial Stabilization Fund; South Korea's Financial Supervisory Service

What changed: On Nov 9, the steps announced Nov 6 took effect: Taiwan's Financial Supervisory Commission cut intraday short sales of borrowed shares to 3% of a stock's average daily volume over the prior 30 trading days (from 30%) and raised the minimum short-sale margin from 90% to 130%, and South Korea exempted brokerages from the 140% minimum collateral ratio on margin loans through Jan 31. Asian markets were mixed that day: MSCI's broadest index of Asia-Pacific shares outside Japan was up 0.8% by midday after falling 4.6% the week before, while South Korea's KOSPI closed 0.8% lower. On Nov 6, Japan's Nikkei had fallen 5.96%, South Korea's KOSPI 6.02% and Taiwan's index 4.5%, Korea's exchange paused trading for 20 minutes under its automatic circuit breaker, and Taiwan's National Financial Stabilization Fund authorized buying stocks, its 10th activation.

The selloff followed the Nov 3 U.S. midterm elections and worries that the results would slow AI and data center spending. The steps aim to support prices: buying stocks, curbing bets on falling prices, and easing margin calls so fewer investors are forced to sell. Several people told Reuters that Taiwan's Finance Ministry asked state-owned banks to support the market. Taiwan's fund cited record foreign selling of NT$994 billion in October and NT$524 billion more in November through Nov 6. None of these steps limits bank withdrawals, currency exchange or moving money abroad.

Why it matters: It shows how quickly governments step into markets during a sharp selloff, including limits on one kind of trade. Steps like these can fuel claims that broader limits on selling or moving money are next.

Why it might not: State stock-buying funds and short-sale curbs are familiar, temporary tools in Asian markets. They don't stop investors from selling shares they own or from moving their money, and the U.S. has no similar state stock fund.

Money market fund rules today: redemption gates are gone, but some funds must charge liquidity fees under heavy outflows

SEC

What changed: No new rule in the window. On Aug 4, 2026 the SEC published a routine paperwork notice on Rule 2a-7, and the 2023 reforms remain in force; their compliance dates passed in 2024.

A federal plan would bar Americans from some investments in tech firms tied to six countries and make them report others. It does not limit normal payments or bank accounts.

Learn moreShow less

Treasury proposes rules for the outbound investment law (COINS Act): a wider list of countries and technologies, with some deals banned and others requiring notice

U.S. Treasury, Office of Investment Security

What changed: Treasury published a proposed rule (91 FR 67396; RIN 1505-AC94; docket TREAS-DO-2026-0595) to implement the COINS Act, which Congress passed in the FY2026 NDAA on Dec 18, 2025. It would create a new 31 CFR Part 851 to replace the Part 850 program that has applied since Jan 2, 2025.

U.S. persons (citizens, green-card holders, U.S. entities and their foreign branches) would have to notify Treasury of some investments, and would be barred from others, in 'covered foreign persons' tied to countries of concern that work in listed technologies. Covered deals include buying equity or contingent equity, certain convertible or equity-like loans, joint ventures, greenfield projects, directing a foreign person's deal, and some limited-partner stakes in non-U.S. funds. U.S. parents would also answer for deals made by foreign subsidiaries they control. The countries of concern grow from China (including Hong Kong and Macau) to also cover Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia and Venezuela 'under the regime of Nicolas Maduro', which Treasury says no longer exists. The technologies grow from semiconductors, quantum and AI (renamed 'super intelligence systems') to add high-performance computing/supercomputing and hypersonics; Treasury's press release also lists biotechnology. The statute exempts bank lending, payment processing and clearing, underwriting, prime brokerage and global custody, plus de minimis deals at amounts Treasury has not yet set. National-interest exemptions are available.

“prohibit United States persons from engaging in certain other transactions”

Why it matters: This is the only live federal framework that requires advance notice for, or outright bans, Americans' investments abroad. It now rests on a statute instead of an executive order, so it is more durable. The proposal widens the countries and sectors covered and lets Treasury add more technology categories through notice and comment. It also reaches indirect routes (fund-of-funds and LP stakes, structures that hide a U.S. person's role) and makes 'reasonable and diligent inquiry' a compliance duty.

Why it might not: It is targeted at national security. It covers only specified technology activities by entities linked to six named countries, and it does not limit ordinary payments, bank lending, custody or deposits. Treasury reports only about 100 notifications in roughly 21 months of the existing program. For most Americans it limits nothing about moving or holding money.

A 1% federal tax on cash-funded remittances abroad has applied since Jan 1, 2026; final regulations are still pending

IRS / Treasury (statute: One Big Beautiful Bill Act, P.L. 119-21, IRC sec. 4475)

What changed: The tax took effect Jan 1, 2026. Proposed regulations were issued Apr 10, 2026, and comments closed June 12. No final regulations were found as of Nov 4.

Senders owe 1% on remittance transfers to recipients abroad when they pay the provider with cash, a money order, a cashier's check or a similar physical instrument; the proposal adds traveler's checks. Transfers funded from a bank or credit-union account, or with a U.S.-issued debit or credit card, are outside the tax. Providers collect the tax, deposit it twice a month, file quarterly on Form 720, and owe the tax themselves if they fail to collect it. The proposal includes an anti-abuse rule that lets the IRS recharacterize transactions structured to avoid the tax.

FDIC proposal would move the largest banks under the 2008 'provisional hold' rule, for use if they fail

FDIC

What changed: The FDIC Board approved the proposal Oct 30; it was published Nov 4, with comments due Jan 4, 2027. It would rescind 12 CFR Part 370 and move banks with at least $2 billion in deposits and 2 million or more accounts under an amended 12 CFR 360.9.

FinCEN renews the Southwest border cash-reporting order at $1,000 for money services businesses

FinCEN (Treasury)

What changed: A new Geographic Targeting Order took effect Sept 3, 2026, following the March 2026 order (2026-04641). Newly covered businesses had to comply by Oct 3, 2026.

U.S. persons (citizens, green-card holders, U.S. entities and their foreign branches) would have to notify Treasury of some investments, and would be barred from others, in 'covered foreign persons' tied to countries of concern that work in listed technologies. Covered deals include buying equity or contingent equity, certain convertible or equity-like loans, joint ventures, greenfield projects, directing a foreign person's deal, and some limited-partner stakes in non-U.S. funds. U.S. parents would also answer for deals made by foreign subsidiaries they control. The countries of concern grow from China (including Hong Kong and Macau) to also cover Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia and Venezuela 'under the regime of Nicolas Maduro', which Treasury says no longer exists. The technologies grow from semiconductors, quantum and AI (renamed 'super intelligence systems') to add high-performance computing/supercomputing and hypersonics; Treasury's press release also lists biotechnology. The statute exempts bank lending, payment processing and clearing, underwriting, prime brokerage and global custody, plus de minimis deals at amounts Treasury has not yet set. National-interest exemptions are available.

Why it matters: This is the only live federal framework that requires advance notice for, or outright bans, Americans' investments abroad. It now rests on a statute instead of an executive order, so it is more durable. The proposal widens the countries and sectors covered and lets Treasury add more technology categories through notice and comment. It also reaches indirect routes (fund-of-funds and LP stakes, structures that hide a U.S. person's role) and makes 'reasonable and diligent inquiry' a compliance duty.

Why it might not: It is targeted at national security. It covers only specified technology activities by entities linked to six named countries, and it does not limit ordinary payments, bank lending, custody or deposits. Treasury reports only about 100 notifications in roughly 21 months of the existing program. For most Americans it limits nothing about moving or holding money.

Details

What would raise the level

Specific next steps to watch for.

  • A cap on, or a government approval requirement for, individual transfers abroad, beyond sanctions and national-security investment screening.
  • H.R. 8995, or any similar bill raising the remittance tax or extending it to bank- and card-funded transfers, getting a hearing, a markup or a ride on a must-pass bill.
  • FinCEN extending cross-border reporting orders to more regions, lowering thresholds, renewing them repeatedly or making them permanent; a new proposal to lower the $3,000 record-keeping and travel-rule threshold for transfers abroad; or a rule implementing FATF's payment-transparency standard that adds source-of-funds or approval steps for ordinary transfers abroad.
  • Any regulator discussing temporary limits on deposit withdrawals or bank holidays, bringing back money-fund redemption gates, or extending liquidity fees to government or retail funds, or officials applying terms like 'temporary liquidity restrictions' or 'capital flow management' to U.S. deposits, stablecoins or money funds.
  • FDIC provisional holds being used at a large bank failure, a cluster of failures in a short period, or several large banks adding unexplained withdrawal or wire limits (confirmed by banks or regulators, not social media).
  • Final stablecoin rules allowing freezes without a specific lawful order, extending freeze duties to all secondary-market holders, restricting self-hosted wallets, or a mandatory (not voluntary) hold regime for crypto withdrawals.
  • Rules that let anyone other than the payer and payee, such as a government or bank acting without a court order, set or override conditions on tokenized deposits.
  • Emergency powers (IEEPA, the new Russia/Iran sanctions law or the Defense Production Act) used against U.S. persons' general transactions, gold or foreign currency, or lifting the EO 14436 waivers with restrictions on U.S. persons.
  • Requirements for retirement accounts, money funds or banks to hold set shares of Treasury debt, or new limits on retirement withdrawals, rollovers, or foreign accounts and assets.
  • Repeal or narrowing of the statutory CBDC bar, a retail CBDC pilot, or a digital euro holding cap cited by U.S. officials as a model.
  • The final outbound-investment rule widening to publicly traded securities, index funds, or more countries or technologies.
  • FinCEN section 9714 or 311 orders reaching widely used U.S. payment apps, stablecoins or exchanges that serve ordinary Americans, rather than offshore firms tied to sanctions evasion.

About this report

What counts: limits on Americans' ability to withdraw, move, convert, invest or hold money. A routine anti-fraud, sanctions, anti-money-laundering or tax-reporting rule isn't a capital control by itself, so each item says what the measure actually lets the government or a bank do.

Control ladder: Visibility (reporting) → Friction (delays, extra checks, fees, taxes) → Permission (government approval needed) → Prohibition (ban, limit, freeze, forced conversion).

Signal tiers: Tier 1 is subtle (new reporting, cash-reporting changes, outbound-investment limits, new official vocabulary, proposed emergency powers, payment systems that enable monitoring or conditions). Tier 2 is concerning (limits on transfers abroad or currency conversion, unexplained withdrawal limits, regulators discussing deposit limits, holding periods or redemption gates, limits on foreign assets, retirement-account limits). Tier 3 is serious (government withdrawal limits, broad bans on moving money overseas, forced conversion, bank holidays, emergency orders).

Alert levels: 🟢 GREEN means quiet. 🟡 YELLOW means subtle movement. 🟠 ORANGE means an official proposal for a Tier 2 measure, or one adopted for a defined group. 🔴 RED means a Tier 3 measure is adopted, imminent or in effect for ordinary Americans. Measures limited to failed banks, sanctioned parties or named countries count toward YELLOW, not ORANGE. Commentary and unverified claims can't raise the level.

How it's made: researched and compiled each morning with an AI assistant from public sources. It can contain errors, so check the linked sources before relying on anything.
